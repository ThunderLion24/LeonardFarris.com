Equality and Freedom

Equality

Are all human beings truly equal? These days, everywhere you go, there is talk about the fight for equality. People demand fairness in opportunities, treatment, and rights, believing that equality is the foundation of justice. As a wise man once said, “Heaven does not create one person above or below another.” People like to use these words to describe that everyone is equal without understanding the full quote. It goes on to say that “while we are all equal at birth, pretty soon, things begin to change.” From the moment we enter the world, circumstances such as family, wealth, education, and environment begin to shape our paths. Academic effort, social connections, and even luck set some people apart to rise above others. Equality, therefore, is not a natural state but an ideal that societies strive for, often without truly achieving it. At any rate, humans change over time based on their actions and the opportunities available to them. Truth be told, at the end of the day, equality is more of a concept than a reality. People are born with different talents, strengths, and weaknesses, and life rewards those who can use them effectively. Yet, society continues to chase equality because it represents hope—the hope that everyone can have a fair chance to succeed regardless of their background. The problem is that equality of opportunity often turns into equality of expectation, which is unrealistic. We live in a world that values merit, competition, and achievement, and these naturally create differences among people. So, while equality is a noble pursuit, it remains an ongoing struggle between what we wish the world to be and what it truly is. True equality, then, is not about making everyone the same but about ensuring that everyone has the same chance to reach their potential. It is about removing unfair barriers, not erasing individuality. While perfect equality may never exist, striving for it pushes humanity toward fairness and compassion. It reminds us that justice is not found in sameness but in giving each person the opportunity to rise as far as their abilities and determination can take them. Equality remains an ongoing struggle between the world as it is and the world as it ought to be.

Freedom

In many countries, citizens are called “free” because they have the right to speak, not because they are heard. This statement highlights the difference between the appearance of freedom and its reality. Having the right to speak means people can express opinions without legal punishment, but being heard means those opinions influence decisions or bring change. In many societies, citizens are told they are free because they can voice dissent, yet their voices are ignored by those in power. True freedom requires not only the ability to speak but also systems that listen, respond, and value what people say. Without that, the central idea of freedom collapses. Freedom is often celebrated as one of the most fundamental rights of human existence. Many nations proudly declare their citizens to be “free,” pointing to the right to speak as proof of that freedom. Yet, reality is far more complex. In many cases, people are called free not because their voices matter, but simply because they are allowed to speak. This distinction reveals a deeper truth about modern societies: the difference between the illusion of freedom and the experience of it. Having the right to speak is only the surface of liberty. It allows individuals to express their thoughts without fear of punishment but does not guarantee that they will be acknowledged or acted on. Being heard, on the other hand, means that one’s voice has weight, that it can influence decisions and inspire change. When governments or institutions allow speech but ignore the people, freedom becomes hollow—a performance meant to maintain the appearance of democracy while silencing genuine participation. True freedom requires more than permission to speak. It demands systems that listen, respond, and value the voices of citizens. A society that only tolerates speech but refuses to hear it is not free; it is merely controlled through the illusion of choice. When people’s words fall on deaf ears, their freedom becomes symbolic rather than meaningful. Therefore, freedom should not be measured by the ability to speak alone, but by the power of speech to create impact. A nation that listens to its people, that allows their voices to shape its future, is one that truly embodies freedom. Without that, the right to speak is nothing more than an empty promise. Without that, freedom is nothing more than an illusion. Without that, the central idea of freedom will collapse.
